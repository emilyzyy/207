# The Autoschedule contract

What Autoschedule promises, written from the implementation and checked against it. Where the
two disagreed, the disagreement is recorded and the resolution says which side moved.

Read this alongside `ScheduleScore`, `ActivityPlacer`, `TravelLegPlanner` and
`AutoScheduleInteractor`; those four hold almost all of the behaviour described here.

---

## 1. What is an activity

An activity is a `ScheduledEvent` of type `ACTIVITY` with a non-null `Activity` behind it. It
carries an identity (`eventId`), a place, a category, an indoor/outdoor flag, and a duration.

**Its duration is fixed.** Scheduling moves activities; it never shortens or lengthens one.
A day that arrives with three sixty-minute activities leaves with three sixty-minute
activities.

**Every activity survives exactly once.** Scheduling is a permutation with times attached, not
a filter. If the day cannot be arranged, Autoschedule refuses and names the obstacle — it does
not quietly drop the activity that would not fit.

## 2. What is a generated travel event

A travel event is derived data: `EventType.TRAVEL`, no `Activity`, generated by Autoschedule to
describe the journey into the activity that follows it. It exists only while the arrangement
that produced it exists.

Consequences, all of which are enforced:

- Travel is generated **between** activities, never before the first or after the last.
- A leg of zero minutes produces **no row**. A row nobody can see is worse than no row.
- Any hand edit to the day — add, remove, retime — **drops every generated travel block**
  (`ScheduleEdits.withoutDerivedTravel`). "Travel to the CN Tower" is only meaningful while
  the CN Tower is still in the plan, in that position, at that time.
- Re-running Autoschedule schedules **activities only**; it never treats a previous run's
  travel rows as things to be scheduled.

## 3. How departure time is selected

Two different questions, deliberately answered separately.

**For feasibility**, the planner asks *how early could I get there* (`TravelLegPlanner.plan`).
It considers every legal departure from the moment the traveller is free, and takes the one
arriving earliest — because travel time depends on departure time, a later departure can
occasionally arrive sooner. This is what decides whether an order is possible at all.

**For the journey actually taken**, the departure is then slid as late as it will go
(`TravelLegPlanner.latestArrivingBy`). The arrival is already fixed; leaving earlier buys
nothing.

> **Rule.** For consecutive activities A and B, when B must begin later than A ends plus travel,
> travel is scheduled *just in time* to reach B — not immediately after A — unless the schedule
> has an explicit reason to model waiting at the destination.

Two things count as an explicit reason:

1. **An unavailable period across the journey.** The traveller is busy 2–3 pm and B starts at
   3 pm: the just-in-time departure would run through the appointment, so the journey lands
   immediately *before* the window instead.
2. **Departure-dependent cost.** Each departure period offers one candidate, priced at that
   period. A candidate is only eligible if the cost used to place it is the cost of departing
   then, so a leg is never priced from one period and travelled in another.

> **Disagreement found.** The implementation only ever had the first question. Travel was
> materialised at the earliest-arrival departure, so a 7-minute hop to a restaurant opening at
> 11:30 was drawn at 10:00 with 83 minutes of standing outside a shut door.
> **Resolved in favour of the contract**: the engine changed.

## 4. Where unavoidable idle time sits

Between the previous activity and the journey — near where the traveller already is — not
between arriving and the doors opening. This follows directly from §3 and is the whole point
of it.

The total amount of dead time is unchanged by this rule. It is *relocated*, not removed.

## 5. How opening hours affect placement

A hard constraint. An activity must fit **entirely inside a single opening window**
(`ActivityPlacer.intoOpeningHours`). A venue open 09:00–11:00 and 15:00–17:00 offers a visitor
two hours twice, never eight hours once.

If no window can hold the visit — shut all day, every window already past, every window shorter
than the visit — placement fails. That is a refusal, not something the schedule may overrun.

Unknown hours are **permissive**: the activity carries one wide window, and behaves exactly as
it did before real hours existed. Parsing lives in the Places adapter; the use case only ever
sees normalised windows.

## 6. How locks and unavailable periods affect placement

**A lock** pins an activity to a fixed window. The scheduler may not move it. It may only
confirm the traveller can still *reach* it: `placeLocked` fails if the earliest possible
arrival is after the locked start. Locks that cannot be honoured produce a named conflict, not
a silently-moved activity.

**An unavailable period** is inviolable and applies to travel as well as activities. Nothing
may sit inside one — a journey may not run through it, and an activity may not overlap it.

## 7. What "minimize gaps" measures

**Avoidable** waiting: time between arriving somewhere and being able to start, minus anything
no ordering could have reclaimed. Excluded are waiting for a venue to open and time inside an
unavailable period. Penalising those would push the search toward worse schedules for no
benefit — nobody can reorder their way out of a restaurant that opens at 11:30.

It is measured from the **earliest possible arrival**, deliberately not from the just-in-time
journey. Setting out later relocates dead time; it does not reclaim it. Scoring the actual
journey would report zero avoidable waiting everywhere and delete this objective entirely.

**This is not the figure the Preview reports.** See §11.

## 8. What "minimize travel" measures

The sum of the minutes actually travelled across every generated leg, at the cost of the
period each leg is actually departed in.

Switching the preference off zeroes the term in the ranking; it does not stop the placer
computing travel (it still needs it to know what is reachable) and does not stop the Preview
reporting it. Only the ranking stops caring, which is what switching a factor off should mean.

## 9. How soft penalties enter the score

Every consideration is expressed in the same unit — minutes the traveller would rather not
spend — and **added**:

```
cost = travel + avoidableIdle + policyPenalties + orderPenalty
```

Adding rather than ranking in tiers is deliberate. With strict tiers a trivial gain in one
consideration could outrank any amount of extra travel, so the schedule could send someone
across the city to shift lunch by ten minutes. Because each soft penalty is **capped**, a soft
improvement can never justify more extra travel than its own cap.

Policies (`MealWindowPolicy`, `DaylightPolicy`, `WeatherSuitabilityPolicy`) reach the engine as
a list chosen by the Interactor. The engine scores whatever list it is handed and never asks
which policy is which, so turning a preference off is a change of input rather than a branch in
the search.

`orderPenalty` is a small capped charge for rearranging the traveller's original order, added
only when they ask to keep it. Capped, so it stays a near-tie consideration rather than
something that can outweigh a genuinely better day.

## 10. How ties are resolved

By a deterministic string of the scheduled event ids (`ScheduleScore.tieBreak`). Equal-cost
schedules always resolve the same way, so the same input produces the same output every time.

Pruning uses **strict** comparison, so a branch that could merely tie is still explored: the
tie-break is not knowable from a partial schedule, and discarding ties would let the answer
depend on the order the tree happened to be walked.

## 11. How the displayed metrics are calculated

**Travel** — before: the journeys the current plan implies, charged with the same estimator the
search uses, so a hand-built day with no travel rows is not reported as costing zero travel.
After: the travel drawn on the proposed timeline.

**Waiting** — *all* the waiting on both sides, from the second activity onwards. Time before
the day's first activity is not waiting; it is a day that starts later.

**Activities moved** — how many proposed start times differ from their current ones.

> **Disagreement found.** "Before" counted every gap while "after" reported only *avoidable*
> waiting (§7). Two different quantities, so the Preview could show `0 min` against a visible
> 83-minute hole and announce "289 min of waiting removed" against a figure that had never
> measured the same thing.
> **Resolved in favour of the contract**: the report changed, the objective did not. Ranking
> still uses avoidable waiting, which is the right measure for choosing between orders and the
> wrong one for describing a day.

**The binding rule**: metrics recomputed from the displayed rows must equal the reported
metrics. Enforced by test, over both the reported scenario and every generated one.

## 12. How missing or failed travel estimates are handled

A failed estimate is a statement about the estimate, never a free journey. Estimates carry a
`TravelEstimateQuality` (`ROUTED`, `ESTIMATED`, `UNKNOWN`), the weakest quality in the matrix is
surfaced as a Preview warning, and a routing failure degrades to a disclosed approximation
rather than to zero.

## 13. How explanations are derived

Every improvement card is a **before/after comparison**, not a description of the result. An
activity already in daylight at 10 am and still in daylight at 2 pm has not been improved.

So each per-activity judgement re-runs *the same policy objects the search used* against the
activity's original time and compares that penalty with the penalty at its proposed time. A
lower penalty is a real improvement, measured by the rule that produced the schedule.
Reimplementing "what counts as daylight" in the explainer would have been a second definition
free to drift from the first.

Only improvements become cards. Trade-offs — travel that grew, waiting that grew, an activity
pushed out of daylight — appear under "Why this schedule?" with the full before/after figures.
A comparison that only ever flatters the feature is not a comparison.

Cards are capped at the strongest four; the rest stay under the disclosure.

## 14. Preview, Apply and Cancel

**Preview** computes and displays; it writes nothing. Repeated previews replace, never
accumulate.

**Cancel** leaves the saved day exactly as it was, and takes *everything describing the
proposal* with it — rows, figures, warnings and cards together. Pins are the traveller's and
outlive a proposal they declined.

**Apply** replaces the schedule atomically, and is refused if the Day Plan changed after the
proposal was made (`ScheduleFingerprint`).

A proposal is **read-only**: while previewing, the cards on the timeline cannot be dragged, and
the per-event Edit and Remove controls are disabled. Until Apply succeeds the itinerary is
untouched.

Background work — notably the hourly forecast — must not disturb an open Preview.

> **Disagreement found.** The forecast worker rebuilt the Day Plan state through a constructor
> that defaults the status to idle, so a forecast arriving mid-Preview silently discarded the
> proposal and the traveller's pins. Cancel did the opposite, keeping the improvement cards for
> a schedule that had just been declined.
> **Resolved in favour of the contract**: both paths changed.

## 15. Layer boundaries

| Concern | Where it lives |
|---|---|
| What time an activity can start; when to set out | `use_case.autoschedule` (engine, placer, leg planner) |
| What the day currently costs; what improved | `use_case.autoschedule` (metrics, improvement finder) |
| Turning a plan into rows, sentences and figures | `interface_adapter.presenters` |
| What is on screen and where | `views` |
| Parsing provider data into normalised windows | `interface_adapter` / infrastructure adapters |

No scheduling decision is made in Swing. No routing or repository call happens inside the
search: travel is prefetched into a matrix first, which is what makes the engine a pure
function of its problem and exhaustively testable.
